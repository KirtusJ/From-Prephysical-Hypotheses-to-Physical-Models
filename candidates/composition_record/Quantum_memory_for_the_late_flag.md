# A smaller quantum memory for a noisy late flag

**Kirtus Justus**, Theoretical Physicist, Port Coquitlam, British Columbia, Canada. Research supplement by Director G (OpenAI Codex).

The candidate's late probe asks whether a covector over \(\mathbb F_5^3\) vanishes on a chosen direction. **Every error-free unassisted quantum memory for this interface needs at least 32 dimensions. If occasional errors are allowed, a 31-dimensional quantum memory achieves mean error \(1/160\), below the optimal \(5/496\) for 31 classical states.** The input is uniform over the 32 distinct binary response classes, and the later setting is an independent uniform projective direction. The gap concerns one late answer. The candidate has supplied neither these coherent preparations and measurements nor observed frequencies. [Classical table, exact thresholds and source model](README.md#a-noisy-test-of-memory-after-the-sources-disconnect) · [Gallego et al., prepare-and-measure dimension tests](https://arxiv.org/abs/1010.5064)

## What perfect decoding requires

There is a blank row and one row for each of the 31 projective covectors. Distinct nonzero rows each vanish on six directions and their two zero sets intersect in one. Hence they differ on ten columns; a nonzero row differs from the blank on 25. The reader chooses its binary measurement after source disconnection. Its complete accessible input state \(\rho_i\) may be mixed, and the effect \(0\le E_q\le I\) can depend arbitrarily on the query. If the two ideal bits differ on \(q\), probability-one and probability-zero answers imply that the two supports lie in the eigenvalue-one and eigenvalue-zero spaces of \(E_q\), respectively. Thus all 32 supported states are pairwise orthogonal in an exact implementation. A diagonal classical encoding attains this minimum. The same reasoning requires 125 dimensions for exact raw scalar answers or for exact binary answers after an arbitrary supplied offset, since those interfaces have 125 distinguishable rows.

Input-independent shared classical randomness cannot lower the exact minimum. With finitely many inputs and queries, probability of success one for each gives a simultaneous full-measure set of good random seeds. The bound counts all source-dependent resources available to the late decoder, including side registers. If only the transmitted subsystem is counted and the decoder keeps half of a prior entangled pair, that is a different resource accounting. For example, two six-dimensional systems in a maximally entangled state allow 36 orthogonal joint encodings after a local cyclic shift and phase on the transmitted system, enough for these 32 inputs. The decoder then uses both systems. [Bennett and Wiesner, entanglement-assisted classical communication](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.69.2881)

## Real 31-dimensional preparations

Write \(N=31\). In \(\mathbb R^{31}\) give the nonzero covectors distinct coordinate vectors \(e_i\), put \(u=N^{-1/2}\sum_i e_i\), and encode the blank by \(u\). Encode any nonzero covector of projective class \(i\) by
\[
 v_i=\sqrt{N/(N-1)}\left(e_i-N^{-1/2}u\right).
\]
The \(v_i\) form a regular simplex in the 30-dimensional subspace perpendicular to \(u\). They have unit norm and \(\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=-1/30\) for distinct \(i,j\). The blank line and the simplex share one 31-dimensional carrier. A complex Hilbert space of the same dimension works with these real preparations. Encoding whole inputs as nonorthogonal states for a later question is related to quantum fingerprinting. The equality problem in the original fingerprint protocol uses a different input and observation from this projective-incidence query. [Buhrman et al., *Quantum Fingerprinting*](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.87.167902)

For a query \(q\), denote its six annihilating nonzero inputs by \(S_q\). Return **zero** on the subspace \(\operatorname{span}(u,\{v_i:i\in S_q\})\) and **nonzero** on its orthogonal complement. The blank and all six annihilating inputs are answered perfectly. For an outside input \(v_j\), every overlap with the six selected vertices equals \(-1/30\). Their Gram matrix has diagonal 1 and off-diagonal \(-1/30\). Its all-ones vector has eigenvalue \(1-5/30=25/30\). Thus the squared length projected onto their span is
\[
 \frac{6(1/30)^2}{25/30}=\frac1{125}.
\]
This is the conditional probability of the sole error, reporting zero on a genuinely nonzero pair. Each query has 25 outside inputs out of 32. The mean error is therefore \((25/32)(1/125)=1/160\), and the maximum conditional error is \(1/125\). In the real ambient coordinates the zero projector for each \(S_q\) acts as the identity on its six coordinates and replaces the complementary 25 coordinates by their common average. Each selected vertex and the blank lie in its range, whose dimension is seven. This rational representation implements the same projection and avoids numerical square roots.

The chosen projectors minimize the **mean** error for these fixed 32 preparations, even among unrestricted binary measurements. The simplex frame operator \(\sum_i v_i v_i^\mathsf T=(31/30)I\) on \(u^\perp\). Put \(A_q=\sum_{i\in S_q}v_i v_i^\mathsf T\). With equal input weights, the operator weighting a correct zero relative to a correct nonzero is proportional on \(u^\perp\) to \(2A_q-(31/30)I\). The nonzero eigenvalues of \(A_q\) coincide with those of its six-vector Gram matrix: \(25/30\) once and \(31/30\) five times. Thus its whole span has strictly positive relative weight and the complement strictly negative weight. Any binary effect has diagonal entries between zero and one in this eigenbasis. Choosing the projector on the whole positive subspace maximizes the linear success score; the blank is also assigned zero with certainty. Optimality over *different* 31-dimensional preparations is open here.

## A comparison that also accounts for randomized classical readers

At fixed classical random seed, a 31-state memory must assign two of its 32 inputs to the same message. At a minimum ten query answers separate that pair. A deterministic decoder incurs at least ten errors among the \(32\cdot31=992\) equally weighted input–setting pairs. Randomized encoders and decoders also satisfy the mean lower bound by fixing all input-independent random coins and averaging. Merging just one closest pair attains \(10/992=5/496\). Since \(1/125<5/496\), even the quantum protocol's **largest** individual error falls below this classical uniform-mean lower bound. The absolute mean-error gap is \(19/4960\). Finite-sample exclusion would require an upper confidence bound on the measured mean below \(5/496\), including misses and any departures from uniform independent sampling. A different distribution on source pairs generally assigns different weights to the 32 response classes. [Exact classical optimum under this prior](memory_errors.json)

For comparison, an unassisted \(d\)-dimensional quantum implementation with uniform mean error \(\epsilon\) obeys the weaker necessary bound
\[
 \epsilon\ge\frac{10}{4\cdot31^2}\left(\frac{32}{d}-1\right)
 \quad (d\le32).
\]
Indeed \(\sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{tr}(\rho_i\rho_j)\ge N_0^2/d-N_0\), \(N_0=32\), by Cauchy–Schwarz for \(\sum_i\rho_i\) and \(\operatorname{tr}\rho_i^2\le1\). For a query distinguishing a pair, let its conditional errors be \(e_i,e_j\). The trace distance \(D_{ij}\) of the two states is at least \(\max(0,1-e_i-e_j)\) from this measurement. The squared root fidelity bounds \(\operatorname{tr}(\rho_i\rho_j)\) from above and is at most \(1-D_{ij}^2\); hence \(\operatorname{tr}(\rho_i\rho_j)\le2(e_i+e_j)\). Average this inequality over each pair's at least ten distinguishing settings, sum over ordered distinct pairs, and bound the sum by allowing every setting on the right. Each input error then occurs at most twice for each of the other 31 inputs. Dividing gives the claimed lower bound. At \(d=31\) the result is \(5/59582\). Together with the achieved \(1/160\) it brackets the optimum but leaves a gap. [Fuchs and van de Graaf, trace distance and fidelity](https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9712042)

**What the compression gives up.** Nonzero multiples of a source covector have the same encoded state. A later *external scalar query* can distinguish them. Even a later controlled additive write cannot act consistently on these projective input states. Take \(v=e_1\) and \(2v\), whose encodings agree. After the same offset \(-v\) and a probe in direction \(e_1\), the first target is zero and the second is nonzero. Any channel applied to identical initial states gives identical statistics; at least one of these two conditional flag errors is then at least \(1/2\). The same example covers state-independent randomized processing or scratch. Implementing the compression as a reversible, reusable writer, or deriving the coherent states and square-rule read from the candidate, requires additional physical input.
