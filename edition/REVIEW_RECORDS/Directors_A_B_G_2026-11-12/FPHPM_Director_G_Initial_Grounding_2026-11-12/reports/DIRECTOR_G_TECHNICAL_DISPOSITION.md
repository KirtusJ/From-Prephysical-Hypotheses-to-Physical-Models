## 3. Exact phase ambiguity: a counterexample with no numerical premise

In a stipulated two-dimensional complex Hilbert space let

\[
 x=(1,0)^T,\quad y=(\sqrt3/2,1/2)^T,\quad z=(\sqrt3/2,i/2)^T.
\]

Every vector is normalized. With $P(a,b)=|\langle a,b\rangle|^2$, the ordered pairwise probabilities are

\[
 P(x,y)=3/4,\quad P(y,z)=5/8,\quad P(z,x)=3/4.
\]

The ordered Bargmann product is $B(x,y,z)=\langle x,y\rangle\langle y,z\rangle\langle z,x\rangle=(9+3i)/16$. Complex conjugation of all three rays preserves every transition probability, including the full Fubini–Study metric $d([u],[v])=\arccos|\langle u,v\rangle|$, but changes $B$ to $(9-3i)/16$. Thus an isometry-invariant function of that metric alone cannot equal the signed Bargmann phase in both configurations. The counterexample embeds in every $\mathbb {CP}^n$ with $n\ge1$. The nonidentification mechanism was also published by Li, Wagner and Zhang (2026); this work **does not claim priority** for it.

The real tangent Levi-Civita connection *is* determined by the Riemannian metric. Nevertheless $\det_{\mathbb C}H$ requires a parallel complex structure $J$. Both $J$ and $-J$ are compatible with the same metric and give conjugate scalar determinants. More sharply, replacing $J$ by $-J$ reverses the induced real orientation by $(-1)^n$ in complex dimension $n$. **For even $n$, a chosen real orientation does not resolve that sign choice.** A signed symplectic/Kähler form, or equivalent independently justified structure, is needed. It must not be put in the prephysical premise without being declared an import.

## 4. Determinant holonomy: what can actually be proved

For the conventional complex projective model, in affine coordinates $z\in\mathbb C^n$ let $q=1+\|z\|^2$ and take the standard Kähler metric

\[
 g_{j\bar k}=\frac{q\delta_{jk}-\bar z_j z_k}{q^2},\qquad
 \det(g)=q^{-(n+1)}.
\]

The tautological line $L=\mathcal O(-1)$ has local Hermitian squared norm $q$, hence $L^*=\mathcal O(1)$ has norm $q^{-1}$. The Euler sequence supplies $\det(T^{1,0}\mathbb {CP}^n)\simeq (L^*)^{\otimes(n+1)}$. The displayed metric determinant shows equality of the natural Chern determinant connection with the connection on $(L^*)^{\otimes(n+1)}$ in these frames. Since Levi-Civita equals the Chern connection on a Kähler tangent bundle, for a loop $\gamma$,

\[
 \det_{\mathbb C}\operatorname{Hol}_{T}(\gamma)
 =\operatorname{Hol}_{L^*}(\gamma)^{n+1}.
\]

For a geodesic polygon with no orthogonal consecutive rays, Pancharatnam transport in the tautological line has holonomy $B^{-1}$ for the chosen ordered-product convention, and so the dual holonomy is $B$. Therefore $\det_{\mathbb C}\operatorname{Hol}_{T}(\gamma)=\big(B/|B|\big)^{n+1}$. The phase, not the nonunit modulus of the raw Bargmann product, is raised to the power. Reversing the order or the definition of $J$ conjugates the identity. The argument is model-internal; the stronger claim that such a scalar is prephysically selected is not supported.

An independent finite check transported tangent vectors around 96 seeded geodesic triangles on the round sphere using ambient Rodrigues rotations (not Schild's ladder). All 96 cases, including largest residual, are retained in `verification/ALL_96_SPHERE_HOLONOMY_TRIALS.csv`. The maximal circular phase residual against $2\arg B$ is $1.53143\times10^{-15}$ radians. Finite floating-point agreement is **not** a proof or an operational quantum observation.

## 5. Pure-state geometry does not fix the mixed-state theory

The classical spin has phase space $S^2$ with round metric, symplectic area form and rotation symmetry. Let its classical statistical states be all Borel probability measures $\mathcal P(S^2)$; its pure states are Dirac measures $\delta_n$. Effects are measurable functions $f:S^2\to[0,1]$ and predictions are $\int f\,d\mu$. Classical disjoint-path filters are additive and therefore have no second- or third-order Sorkin interference.

As a stipulated geometric comparison, identify the pure sphere with the pure-state manifold $\mathbb {CP}^1$ and scale the round metric to match Fubini–Study. Both then have holonomy $SO(2)$ with real commutant $\{aI+bJ\}\cong\mathbb C$ and the nonzero area two-form. But the classical statistical state space is a (generally infinite-dimensional) simplex, not the qubit density-matrix ball. In particular, the uniform sphere measure $\mu_{\rm unif}$ and the two-pole mixture $\mu_{\pm z}=\tfrac12(\delta_{+z}+\delta_{-z})$ both have zero mean Bloch vector and hence yield the **same** qubit density matrix $I/2$ under the affine barycenter map $\mu\mapsto\int P_n\,d\mu$. A classical effect $f(n)=n_z^2$ distinguishes them: $\int f\,d\mu_{\rm unif}=1/3$ but $\int f\,d\mu_{\pm z}=1$.

**Boundary of the countermodel:** the statistical simplex has infinite affine dimension, and its maximally distinguishing classical effects do not induce the qubit transition-probability metric. Hence it defeats the broad, unqualified uniqueness proposal, but does **not** defeat a *different* finite-dimensional, Jordan-classified or canonically operational-metric theorem. Barnum–Müller–Ududec (2014) explicitly require additional principles beyond $I_3=0$ to obtain Jordan models; their theorem is not a prephysical derivation of those principles. The narrowed candidate remains conditional and open to direct verification from G7 and independent sources.

## 6. Real qutrit versus complex qubit: a more direct geometric control

For real three-level rays the pure space is $\mathbb {RP}^2$ with distance $d([u],[v])=\arccos|u\cdot v|$. This is locally the unit sphere, so $K=1$. For complex two-level rays the pure space $\mathbb {CP}^1$ with the same normalized projective-distance rule is the round sphere of radius $1/2$, so $K=4$. The numerical curvature result was also recomputed *symbolically* from conformal local metrics $4(dx^2+dy^2)/(1+r^2)^2$ and $(dx^2+dy^2)/(1+r^2)^2$, respectively. Both spaces have the same diameter $\pi/2$ and real dimension two; the factor-of-four Gaussian curvature is a genuine differential discriminator **if that complete metric is supplied**.

Likewise, $\pi_1(\mathbb {RP}^2)=\mathbb Z_2$, its manifold is nonorientable and its full Levi-Civita holonomy is $O(2)$; by contrast $\pi_1(\mathbb {CP}^1)=0$, it is orientable and its full holonomy is $SO(2)$. Only their *restricted* holonomy commutants coincide with $\mathbb C$. Monodromy is valuable, but it is not literally the sole differentiator. None of these observations identifies how the metric arises from a prephysical system.

## 7. Finite homogeneous carriers: precise hypotheses and two covering bounds

Gelander's 2013 Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.3 give a directly relevant obstruction: any **Gromov–Hausdorff limit of finite homogeneous metric spaces** that is a manifold must be a torus. A connected complex-projective pure space $\mathbb {CP}^n$, $n\ge1$, is not a torus. Therefore a proposed finite-carrier limit that preserves *exact transitive isometry symmetry at every stage* cannot yield that target under this convergence. This rules out one route, not all discrete emergence, sampling, or symmetry-restoration routes. It is a statement from an externally published theorem, not a proof rederived here.

Independently, consider a *single orbit* $S$ under a finite isometry subgroup of the round Bloch sphere. If $N=|S|$ and its geodesic covering radius is $r$, the area of $N$ spherical caps implies

\[
 N\,2\pi(1-\cos r)\ge4\pi,\quad r\ge \arccos(1-2/N).
\]

A finite subgroup of $SO(3)$ is cyclic, dihedral, tetrahedral, octahedral or icosahedral. For the last three, the orbit has at most $60$ points; hence $r\ge\arccos(29/30)\approx14.83511^\circ$. Cyclic or dihedral orbits are contained in at most two latitude circles about their invariant axis (apart from small exceptional groups already bounded by cardinality), giving the stronger $r\ge45^\circ$. Thus the first inequality is universal for *unitary-induced $SO(3)$ actions*. If antiunitary symmetries enlarge the acting group to a finite subgroup $G\le O(3)$, its rotation subgroup $H=G\cap SO(3)$ has index at most two. If $H$ is tetrahedral, octahedral or icosahedral, then $|G|\le 120$, so $r\ge\arccos(59/60)\approx10.47531^\circ$. If $H$ is cyclic or dihedral with a unique invariant rotation axis, normality of $H$ forces all of $G$ to preserve that axis as an unoriented line, leaving the orbit on at most two latitudes and giving $r\ge45^\circ$. The exceptional small-axis cases, including $H\simeq D_2$, have $|G|\le8$ and satisfy the same claimed $10.47531^\circ$ bound by the cap-area argument. These separate cases are essential because cyclic and dihedral groups have unbounded order. It is **not** legitimate to use a 120-element binary icosahedral group as 120 distinct unitary-induced Bloch-sphere rotations, since its projective action has at most 60 elements.

The supplied Director Z summary describes an uncovered cap as “10.48°”; an exact $\arccos(59/60)$ bound is approximately $10.47531^\circ$, which rounds to $10.48^\circ$ but does not mathematically certify the **strict** inequality $r\ge10.48^\circ$. Whether Z1 used the unitary, full-Wigner or another symmetry convention is uninspected. Preserve the quoted result as submitted provenance, rather than attributing a mistake to an unavailable proof.
