# Common clocks and relative gains in a masked ring walk

Director G research note for Kirtus Justus · 15 November 2026 · B.C. Pacific Time (PCT, UTC−07:00)

Two preparation schedules may each reach a specified site and rail, yet their gain labels may constrain the times at which they can do so. This note determines when a finite collection of schedules can share a clock, then asks what a measurement can learn from those labels.

## When absolute labels can share a clock

Let a ring have $N\ge3$ sites. Start at site zero on rail R with identity gain. A right or left step moves one site and sets the new rail. Let $a,b,c,d$ be the gains for RR, RL, LR and LL in a finite abelian group. Each tick permits a chosen set of whole sites on both rails. A word can be isolated by retaining only its site at each tick, since the two next sites are distinct. Use elapsed time in integer ticks with no cutoff on the padding time.

Form the finite directed graph with state (site, rail, accumulated gain). Let $p$ be the gcd of the positive return times to the full launch state. **A finite family of schedules with nonempty target stocks at named sites and rails can be run at one common time with their respective absolute stocks preserved exactly if their original lengths are congruent modulo $p$.** Each preparation is a separate run with its own masks. The statement controls clock, site, rail, gain, and multiplicity. Winding can change.

For sufficiency, neutral closed words give singleton prefixes that restore the launch state. Neutral return lengths are closed under addition. Finitely many of them already have gcd $p$. Their additive semigroup contains every sufficiently large multiple of $p$. To see the latter, divide by the gcd and choose one generating length $m$. The residues generated modulo $m$ form the full cyclic group. Choose a nonnegative representative in the semigroup for each residue, then add multiples of $m$. Given compatible original clocks, one sufficiently late common clock therefore works for every schedule.

For necessity, every reachable state has a return path to launch. First close its site and rail. The resulting closed spatial word has gain of finite order, so repeating that word enough times cancels it. Any two paths to one specified state can consequently be continued by the same return path. Subtracting their closed lengths shows that the two path lengths agree modulo $p$. Even replacing an entire schedule with a new preparation cannot change its residue if it retains any named occupied site, rail and absolute gain. An empty stock places no such constraint and can be realized by an empty mask.

### Computing the period

Set $h=bc/(ad)$ and take the quotient of the gain group by the subgroup generated by $h$. Write $u$ and $v$ for the respective images of $a^N$ and $bc$ in this quotient. Then

$$p=\gcd\{Nk+2j:\ k,j\in\mathbb Z,\quad u^k v^j=1\}.$$

Here is a derivation. In the group generated by pairs (integer length, gain) of closed R-to-R words at site zero, $R^N$ contributes $(N,a^N)$ and $LR$ contributes $(2,bc)$. The words $LRLR$ and $LLRR$ return to that site and rail with equal duration. The ratio of their gains is $h$. Conversely, a closed word with $r$ right steps, $j$ left steps, and $n$ L-to-R transitions has $r-j=Nk$ and gain $a^r d^j h^n=(a^N)^k(bc)^j h^{n-j}$. These three pairs generate the whole group of differences. Any formal difference of closed words with the same gain can be represented as a difference of two actual neutral words. Append to both an identical closed word whose gain is the inverse (a power of either word suffices). This yields the gcd above. It includes topological restrictions even when all gains are trivial.

On five sites with all arc labels equal to $-1$, the period is two. $R$ and $R^6$ each reach site one on R, with opposite gains. The clock parity prohibits preparing both absolute labels at one clock. A three-site control separates common time from common winding. When each right step flips the sign and each left step preserves it, the words LLLLRLRR and LRLRRRRR both end at site one on R at time eight, with gains $-1,+1$ and lifts $-2,4$. Fixing time and lift would fix the right-step count and force their signs to agree.

The exact verifier compares the formula with a traversal of the graph, using elapsed-length differences on its directed edges. It checked all 15,186 combinations of ring sizes 3 through 8 and arc quadruples over the trivial group, cyclic groups of orders 2 through 5, and the products $C_2\times C_2$ and $C_2\times C_3$. It traversed 778,078 reachable states and 1,556,156 edges. This verifies the implementation on those inputs. The mathematical argument covers the finite abelian case in the statement.

## What a detector could distinguish

G10 Lemma 5.59 assumes a homogeneous finite abelian gain model, a site-independent rule on each rail, zero weight for an empty multiset and its factor-two continuation identity. A stock can be isolated on one rail. The identity then expresses its value as the mean of its two translated successors. The finite reachable set is connected under these translations. The maximum principle for this finite averaging equation forces the value to be constant on the translation orbit. In particular, multiplying all gains in an occurring right-rail stock by any $h'\in H=\langle a,d,bc\rangle$ preserves its reading. This applies to both stocks in the five-site example. Z confirmed that the paper compares separate events only up to such a factor. For a sharper comparison, take the real normalized Hadamard walk, with transition sign negative only for LL and step magnitude $1/\sqrt2$. Start at (0,R) on five sites. After four masked steps read (0,L). In both settings the same reference history RLRL survives. The binary position masks below specify which sites pass, in time order. Both rails pass at every retained site. At each of the four ticks, an implementation must preserve coherence between permitted histories if using the coherent prediction. In particular, attenuation outside a set is different from recording which of two permitted sites a path occupied.

| Setting | Site masks | Surviving signal | Reference | Coherent port weight | Fully dephased port weight |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| Constructive | {1,4}, {0}, {1}, {0} | LRRL (+) | RLRL (+) | 1/4 | 1/8 |
| Destructive | {1}, {0,2}, {1}, {0} | RRLL (−) | RLRL (+) | 0 | 1/8 |

Each isolated path gives 1/16 under either rule. In a coherent run the two signed amplitudes add before squaring. In a dephased run the individual intensities add. All weights in the table are per input launch, including losses. Conditioning on passage through the first three masks changes the constructive coherent fraction to 1/2. For a mixture with a fraction $\theta$ of coherent runs and the rest fully dephased, the two unconditional weights are $(1+\theta)/8$ and $(1-\theta)/8$. This interpolation names one explicit rival family. Other noise and readout laws remain possible.

The two interventions differ in their early masks, before recombination. They do not implement a common later operation on two completed singletons. A sign applied to all histories of either entire event still cancels from the squared reading. Four ticks are the earliest example on this five-site ring with three distinct histories at one terminal port that can play the reference and two signal roles. The checker tests that restricted minimality by enumeration. It computes the table independently by full word enumeration and propagation of both amplitudes and dephased intensities on the two rails.

The experiment by Xue, Qin and Tang used a one-dimensional photonic walk with an addressed phase defect, providing an experimental precedent for path-dependent control. It used a different apparatus and measured a different observable. Our calculation assumes the coin, coherent masks and squared port readout. The two gains become physically testable only after a physical realization and calibration of those operations. The conditional-control result of Araújo et al. also distinguishes access to an unknown operation from access to a coherent bypass of that operation.
