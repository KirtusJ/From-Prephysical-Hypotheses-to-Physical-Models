# Transporting a walk bin to four integer amplitudes

**Kirtus Justus**, Theoretical Physicist, Port Coquitlam, British Columbia, Canada. Research supplement by Director G (OpenAI Codex).

At 32 ticks the nonnegative half of a signed Hadamard flight has integer squared norm 3,262,634,697 before division by $2^{32}$. Its 33 reachable rail coordinates can be transformed reversibly to $(57119,232,26,6)$ using 41 data coordinates. Four of the 41 coordinates supply an intermediate sum of squares. The shape of the known input determines the circuit. The recorded bin value can stay fixed. This construction addresses the space cost of the [earlier equal-cell reduction](Stable_flattening_inside_a_recorded_bin.md). The four final amplitudes are unequal, so their occupancy counts do not directly give a binary probability.

## Two reflections suffice

Let $x,y$ be nonzero integer vectors of equal squared norm and equal odd part $g$ of their coordinate gcds. Divide both by $g$ and write $P$ for the resulting squared norm. Each row now generates the unit ideal over $D=\mathbb Z[1/2]$. Put the two rows on disjoint named coordinate sets by padding with zeros. Write $\Delta=2^{\lfloor\log_2 P\rfloor}$ and $b=P-\Delta$. Thus $0\le b<P/2$. It suffices to construct a dyadic vector $z$ with

$$
\|z\|^2=P,\qquad x\cdot z=y\cdot z=b .
$$

Under these conditions $w=x-z$ has squared norm $2\Delta$ and $x\cdot w=\Delta$. The dyadic Householder matrix $I-ww^{\mathsf T}/\Delta$ sends $x$ to $z$. The same construction with $w=z-y$ sends $z$ to $y$. Both reflections are involutions and restore the original input when replayed in reverse order. Linearity restores the common factor $g$. A zero-norm bin stays empty.

For the source coordinates, use extended Euclid to construct $c_x\in D^n$ with $x\cdot c_x=1$. The ordinary integer gcd of the divided row is a power of two, so division of its integer Bézout coefficients by that gcd suffices. Round each entry of $(b/P)x$ to a nearest point on the $1/G$ grid, giving a vector $r$, where $G$ is a power of two. Set

$$
  p=r+(b-x\cdot r)c_x .
$$

Then $x\cdot p=b$ exactly. Repeat with $y$ to obtain $q$ on the other support. As $G$ increases, the sum of their squared norms approaches $2b^2/P<P/2$. Stop when that sum is strictly below $P$. A quantitative choice is available. With $n$ an upper bound for the length of either row, $C$ the larger Euclidean norm of their Bézout vectors, and $G\ge 2\sqrt n(1+\sqrt P C)$, the rounding error after repair has norm at most $1/4$ on each support. It is orthogonal to that row, whose ideal target vector is parallel to the row. Hence the sum of squared norms is at most $2b^2/P+1/8<P$. Logarithmic grid doubling finds a successful grid without evaluating square roots.

Choose a common power-of-two denominator $D_0$ for $p,q$. The remaining squared norm is a positive integer divided by $D_0^2$. Write its numerator as a sum of four integer squares, divide those four integers by $D_0$, and append them to $(p,q)$ to obtain $z$. Thus two inputs of widths $n_x,n_y$ need at most **$n_x+n_y+4$ data coordinates** before binary padding. The construction requires blank slots inside the same protected sector. Multiple bins may use independent registers or appropriately partitioned slots.

## Finding the reference and compiling the reflections

If the target is to have at most four components, we need a representation of $P$ by four squares with **odd content one**. Any sum-of-four-squares algorithm may return a row with a common odd factor. For example $9=3^2$ has the needed norm while $(2,2,1,0)$ has the required content. Here is how to obtain an appropriate reference in expected polynomial time.

Use Pollack and Treviño's unconditional randomized algorithm for four squares, with one choice in the restoration of small prime factors. For odd reduced input, its main step obtains, after left multiplication by a Hurwitz unit to give integral coordinates, a Hurwitz quaternion $\alpha$ as a greatest common right divisor of the desired norm and a quaternion $\xi$ with coprime integer coordinates. The divisor's norm is the desired integer. If every integral component of $\alpha$ were a multiple of an odd prime $\ell$, the original quaternion $\xi=\delta\alpha$ would belong to $\ell\mathcal H$. An integral-coordinate element of $\ell\mathcal H$ has every coordinate divisible by $\ell$, since a Hurwitz integer has either integral or half-odd coordinates and $\ell$ is odd. This contradicts primitivity. The algorithm's bounded small cases can instead use a fixed primitive representation, for example by enumeration after checking the finite cases. [Pollack and Treviño, Lemma 2 and §4](https://www.pollack-math.net/finding4squares.pdf)

In preprocessing, their algorithm removes powers of two and small primes $\ell\equiv1\bmod4$. To restore one such prime, write $\ell=a^2+h^2$ as in their algorithm. Given the current odd-primitive quaternion $u$, try $u(a+hi)$ and $u(a-hi)$. Both have norm increased by the factor $\ell$. They cannot both vanish componentwise modulo $\ell$, since their sum is $2au$ and $2a$ is invertible modulo $\ell$. Use either that remains nonzero. At any other odd prime the new factor has invertible norm, so it cannot introduce a common divisor there. Repetition handles prime powers without factoring the large residual norm. Multiplication by $1+i$ restores each removed factor of two and is invertible modulo every odd prime. This supplies an odd-primitive four-square target for every positive $P$. Multiplying the final row by the original bin content $g$ gives a reachable reference for that bin. A requirement of odd content one on the original numerator scale excludes precisely those nonempty bins whose content exceeds one.

Let $L=\lceil\log_2(P+1)\rceil$, $n=\max(2,n_x,n_y)$. In the successive integer extended-gcd steps the size of old coefficients can multiply by at most $2+\sqrt P$ at each new input. The bit lengths of the resulting Bézout coefficients, of $G$, and of the denominators and numerators used to produce $z$, are therefore $O(n(L+1)+\log n)$. The additional common denominator from division by an ordinary power-of-two gcd contributes at most $O(L)$. The slack numerator also has polynomial bit length. The cited unconditional four-square algorithm uses expected polynomially many bit operations for each of the target and padding calls. All other steps use polynomially many operations on polynomial-bit integers, with exact gcd and sum-of-squares checks.

Each reflection normal has the form $w=h/2^a$ and $\|w\|^2=2\Delta$. Thus the squared integer norm of $h$ is an exact power of two, $2^k$ with $k=O(n(L+1)+\log n)$. To compile it on $d$ coordinates rounded up to an even dimension that is a power of two, first handle odd $k$ with a full-register Hadamard on the last address bit. Its integer sum/difference numerator doubles the norm, making the new squared-norm exponent even. The normalized direction is unchanged up to this known Hadamard. At positive even exponent, the number of odd integer components is divisible by four. Group them in fours and choose signs so that each is 1 mod 4. Applying $H\otimes H$ to each quartet gives four even integers at the old scale. Divide **all** numerators by two, updating the common scale without a physical gate. After $O(k+1)$ levels the normal lies on one signed basis coordinate. Conjugating a single sign on that coordinate by the reduction implements the desired reflection on the entire space. An overall sign on the normal has no effect. This is one-vector synthesis applied to the two normals, whose squared lengths our construction fixes in advance. [Amy et al., unit-vector reduction and exact circuit costs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11305)

The two words together take $O(d(k+1))$ addressed signs, swaps, four-coordinate Walsh gates and register-wide Hadamards. On a binary address register the exact elementary-gate bound is $O(d\log(2d)(k+1))$ with the additional clean synthesis workspace of the addressed gates. Serial depth is bounded by this count. These are addressable-circuit estimates. Nearest-neighbor routing, a physical clock and propagation through a real detector impose their own costs. For example, each step moving amplitudes by at most one lattice site cannot transport a nonzero component to a disjoint patch in less than their separation when no other amplitudes reach the output patch. A locality test on extended states requires an actual light-cone mass bound.

There is also a fully deterministic polynomial-bit alternative if more than four final entries are acceptable. Give the reference one entry equal to 1. Write $P-1$ in base four, including the digit's number of copies of $2^j$ for each place $j$. Their squares fill exactly the remaining norm and the first entry ensures odd content one. Use the same base-four rule on the integer slack, without the added one. The reference now has $O(L+1)$ entries, the padding uses $O(n(L+1)+\log n)$ entries, and the same polynomial bit-length and compilation arguments apply. This variant avoids the randomized arithmetic searches. It gives a polynomial-size listing even when $P$ is exponential in the flight length.
