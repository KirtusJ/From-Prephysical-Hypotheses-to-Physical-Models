# What a detector sees

Imagine a ring of sites. At each tick, routes arriving from the right and left can leave in either direction. A route gets a minus sign each time it makes a left-to-left transition. At the end, one detector reports a site and an incoming direction. At the rightmost reachable site there is just one route. Two sites behind that front, many different routes reach the right-facing detector with the same sign. After \(n+1\) ticks their signed total is \(n\), and the neighboring left-facing detector has total one. The growing rim of a single expanding walk supplies a calibration series.

The candidate gives one physical marker to each run. At a site the marker can move to either of that site’s two outgoing detectors. There is nowhere else for it to go that tick. Repeating the launch and choosing when to read therefore allows one to compare a site’s earlier frequency with the combined frequencies of its two outgoing detectors. The comparison uses different runs so that reading a marker early does not affect the later sample.

The more vulnerable assumption concerns what information predicts the marker’s terminal frequency. The candidate says that one rule scores each detector by a single integer, the sum of the signs of all its arriving routes. A positive route and a negative route can cancel. If the apparatus keeps a bit distinguishing them, it may see two distinguishable arrivals instead. Four ticks give a direct comparison: two detectors on the same rail with the same signed total receive different numbers of routes. Equality of their click rates tests the signed-total assumption. Counting routes predicts a three-to-one ratio.

Local marker transport and the common scoring rule together force one route-unit to score one, two units to score four, and \(n\) units of either sign to score \(n^2\). The sign changes matter at later steps, even when they give the same present score. A separate preparation hypothesis lets a long, slowly varying packet on this walk approach the free Schrödinger equation; a further scaling yields a classical position moving at constant velocity. Which apparatus can prepare the packet, how it measures several positions in one run, and how three-dimensional spacetime or a force arises remain physical questions.
