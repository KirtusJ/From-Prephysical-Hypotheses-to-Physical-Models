# Four phase settings and the probability of a rail record

**Kirtus Justus**, Theoretical Physicist, Port Coquitlam, British Columbia, Canada. Research supplement by Director G (OpenAI Codex).

On two wells the walker changes well at each tick regardless of its rail. At a fixed terminal well ask how often one detector event occurs. Assume the coherent controls identify pure, fully prepared rail states with the unit sphere, with unitary rail gates acting by their usual rotations. This is a kinematic assumption about preparations and transformations. The probability of the event can initially be **any bounded Borel function** $F(s)\in[0,1]$ of the prepared point. In particular no trace rule, effect matrix, covariance of detector response, or formula for the probability of a path has been assigned. The question is what measured comparisons can determine $F$.

## A four-setting experiment

Start from the R rail, apply a chosen preparation $A$, then apply a phase $D_{i^j}=\operatorname{diag}(1,i^j)$ for $j=0,1,2,3$, follow with a common coherent rotation $V$ and read the same detector event. Compare the mean event probability on settings $0,2$ with the mean on settings $1,3$. Assign equal weights within each pair, either by stratifying settings and averaging rates, or using an independent fair switch. Use original-launch denominators, including losses. The four pure output points have equal axial latitude about $V z$ and successive azimuths differing by $\pi/2$. They form a Euclidean square inscribed on the unit sphere. Conversely any such square is obtained by choosing $V$ and $A$.

With the switched two-well ticks $U_\phi=D_{e^{i\phi}}H$, a plain phase plate costs two ticks, $U_\phi U_0=D_{e^{i\phi}}$. Each $H$ costs one, and phases about the complementary axis follow by conjugating with $H$. Euler rotations and inverse controls therefore have finite words if the phase angles in the words are available. Use the same compiled $A,V$ for all four branches, including two ticks for the identity phase, so that the terminal well and time agree. Comparing different squares additionally requires that clock and hidden apparatus history do not change the response $F$. No mid-run measurement, projection update, heralded scalar probability or conditional postselection enters these four deterministic schedules. A fixed gain on its own does not supply an adjustable phase plate. The walk's existing density-of-control argument in one nontrivial sheet sector provides only limiting directions, and its recorded excursion success requires independent justification when varying the probability rule. Suppose the absolute discrepancy between the two means is at most $\epsilon$ for every square. At zero discrepancy the possible detector laws are exactly
$$F(s)=\mu+v\cdot s,\qquad |v|\le\min(\mu,1-\mu).$$
If opposite sharp rails $z,-z$ yield probabilities one and zero, respectively, this gives $F(s)=(1+z\cdot s)/2$. For a spinor with that Bloch vector the expression equals its squared R amplitude by the geometric coordinate formula. With allowances for imperfect comparisons this experiment also bounds departures from every affine detector description as explained below. This is a two-level determination from classical mixtures of two settings. Busch's qubit theorem assumes a probability measure on effects, and the program's higher-dimensional string argument uses full projective measurement frames. Here the input equalities concern the measured responses to specified preparations. Whether the two mixtures are in fact operationally equivalent for a physical detector is a substantive test. ## From square comparisons to the response function

Fix any unit vector $a$. Let $h_a(u)$ be the mathematical average of $F$ under rotations about $a$ at latitude $a\cdot s=u$. At the poles $h_a(1)=F(a)$ and $h_a(-1)=F(-a)$. Rotating each square rigidly and averaging shows that the function $s\mapsto h_a(a\cdot s)$ obeys the same $\epsilon$ comparison for every square. This integration is in the proof. No uniform spherical sampling apparatus is needed for the implication.

For $u\ne0$ put $b=\operatorname{sgn}(u)$; take $b=1$ at zero. Choose a unit square axis $n$ with $n\cdot (b a)=\sqrt{|u|}$. Take the first vertex $s_0=b a$ and obtain the others by consecutive quarter turns about $n$. The axial coordinates $a\cdot s_j$ in order are $b,u,2u-b,u$. Indeed the $j$th scalar product is $b[|u|+(1-|u|)\cos(j\pi/2)]$. Consequently
$$\left|h_a(u)-\tfrac12\bigl[h_a(b)+h_a(2u-b)\bigr]\right|\le\epsilon.\tag{1}$$
Let $L_a(u)=[(1+u)F(a)+(1-u)F(-a)]/2$. It satisfies (1) exactly. The absolute error $e_a=|h_a-L_a|$ vanishes at the endpoints and obeys $e_a(u)\le\epsilon+e_a(2u-b)/2$. Thus after $k$ successive comparisons along this orbit,
$$e_a(u)\le 2(1-2^{-k})\epsilon+2^{-k}e_a(u_k).\tag{2}$$
Boundedness and iteration give $e_a(u)\le2\epsilon$ without a smoothness assumption. For a dyadic $u$ the orbit hits an endpoint in finitely many steps (zero requires one more step). Unequal errors along an orbit contribute $\sum_{j<k}2^{-j}\epsilon_j$. For an already axial detector $F(s)=h_z(z\cdot s)$, only the squares starting at $\pm z$ used in (1) are needed for this certificate. A finite orbit of raw settings for a general detector does not directly measure the azimuthal average.

With normalized sphere area $d\sigma$, define $\mu=\int F(s)d\sigma(s)$ and $v=3\int sF(s)d\sigma(s)$. The coordinate $u=a\cdot s$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$ under $d\sigma$. The affine fit at $a$ is therefore $\mu+v\cdot a=\int_{-1}^1 (1+3u)h_a(u)\,du/2$. The same functional applied to $L_a$ equals $L_a(1)=F(a)$, since its zeroth and first moments are both one. The mean of $|1+3u|$ on this interval is $5/3$ by splitting at $-1/3$. Using (2) gives the pointwise bound
$$\sup_{a\in S^2}|F(a)-(\mu+v\cdot a)|\le10\epsilon/3.\tag{3}$$
The fitted affine function in (3) may leave $[0,1]$ by the indicated error. For exact comparisons, positivity on the full sphere implies the stated bound on $|v|$; conversely all affine laws pass, since opposite-pair midpoints agree. With exact sharp calibration, $\mu=1/2$ and $v\cdot z=1/2$ then force $v=z/2$. An uncalibrated constant or tilted response also passes every square. Detector orientation comes from the named sharp preparations.

For imperfect sharp calibration suppose $F(z)\ge1-\eta$ and $F(-z)\le\eta$. Set $\delta=10\epsilon/3$. Equation (3) and $0\le F\le1$ imply $|\mu-1/2|\le\eta/2+\delta$, $|v|\le1/2+\delta$, and $v\cdot z\ge1/2-\eta-\delta$. Hence $|v-z/2|^2\le\eta+2\delta+\delta^2$ and the error from the sharp R law is bounded by $\min(1,\eta/2+2\delta+\sqrt{\eta+2\delta+\delta^2})$ uniformly. A perfectly affine detector pointing along a unit vector $w$ with $w\cdot z=1-2\eta$ has endpoint errors $\eta$ and worst error $|w-z|/2=\sqrt\eta$. Thus sharp-endpoint errors alone generally lead to square-root uncertainty in orientation.

For a finite collection of prepared vertices one may choose a small open spherical cap avoiding them and the calibrated poles and add a continuous bump supported there to an ordinary sharp response, small enough to stay within probability bounds. All tests in that collection then pass exactly. A uniform modulus of continuity supplied independently would permit a finite net to control the untested squares. Borel measurability alone does not: a single untested point can be altered even if a countable dense set of prepared vertices has been tested. Such a point change can violate one of the omitted square comparisons.

## A single common-clock discriminator

Take $\alpha=\pi/4$, $A=H D_{e^{i\alpha}} H$, $V=A^{-1}$, and read R after each of the four phase settings. Every trial lasts ten switched-walk ticks from a localized R input and ends at the initial well. The final Bloch $z$ coordinates, computed from the *coherent rotations*, are $(1,1/2,0,1/2)$. The sharp ordinary law gives probabilities $(1,3/4,1/2,3/4)$, so the mean in each pair is $3/4$. The different covariant law $F(s)=[1+(z\cdot s)^3]/2$ gives $(1,9/16,1/2,9/16)$, with discrepancy $3/16$ per original launch. It is a normalized, bounded, sharp binary response with the correct antipodal exchange and fair mixtures. This one panel separates those two laws. A biased unaccounted switch can change its means even for the ordinary law; a programmed balanced comparison of the separate frequencies removes this confound.

Restricting the Ramsey experiment to squares on great circles loses information. Every response with $F(s)+F(-s)=1$ gives pair mean $1/2$ on **all** such circles. Squares on translated as well as centered planes supply the comparisons in (1). On the nominal Clifford preparations of the existing ion tables the $z$ components are only $-1,0,1$. Every odd scalar law with those calibrated values, including the cubic one, agrees at every such preparation. The ten-tick discriminator requires a non-Clifford tilt. The checker retains this null control, imperfect fair switching and a finite-panel countermodel. Unequal postselection can also change the comparison. In the ordinary ten-tick example, retaining all $j=0$ launches and half the $j=2$ launches independently of their rail gives a pooled R frequency of $5/6$ among retained even settings, although the equal-weight mean is $3/4$. The experiment determines a probability law **relative to a specified sphere of coherent preparations**. A response depending on a hidden preparation history, rotation errors, inaccessible phases or unequal samples across settings can violate its premises. Global conjugation preserves the experiment when it transforms source, controls and detector together. Neither the physical realization of that sphere and phase reference nor probabilities for arbitrary many-body preparations follow from a single rail detector.
